Reason

Reasoning with certainty, reasoning with uncertainty, and the most common mistake: treating “this explains it” as “this is the only answer.”

1. Reasoning with certainty

Start with statements that are simply true or false. Call two of them p and q. Every compound statement is defined by when it is true, and it’s easiest to remember each one by the single case that makes it come out differently.

StatementThe rule to rememberp=T q=TT FF TF F
not ptrue only when p is falseFT
p and qtrue only when both are trueTFFF
p or qfalse only when both are falseTTTF
p implies qfalse only when p is true and q is falseTFTT
p equals qp and q are true or false togetherTFFT

Look at the “p implies q” row. When q is true, the implication holds whether p is true or false. Knowing q tells you nothing about p. Nearly every mistake in these notes comes back to that one column.

There are only two valid ways to use an implication:

  • Modus ponens: p implies q, and p is true, so q is true.
  • Modus tollens: p implies q, and q is false, so p is false.

Two moves look just like these but are invalid:

  • Affirming the consequent: p implies q, and q is true, “so p is true.”
  • Denying the antecedent: p implies q, and p is false, “so q is false.”

2. Three ways to reason

Charles Sanders Peirce (1878) separated reasoning into three kinds by which part of an argument you know and which part you conclude. His example was a bag of beans; here it is next to a sudoku version.

Deduction certain

Rule + case → result

  1. Every row holds 1, 2, 3, 4 once.
  2. This row already has 3, 4 and 2.
  3. So the empty cell is 1.

If the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. It adds no new information; it makes visible what the premises already contained.

Induction probable

Cases + results → rule

  1. I solved 50 puzzles using only “one candidate left.”
  2. Every one worked.
  3. So that move solves every puzzle.

The conclusion goes beyond the evidence. One counterexample ends it, and puzzle B below is one.

Abduction plausible

Rule + result → case

  1. A solution satisfies every row, column and box.
  2. My filled grid satisfies every row, column and box.
  3. So my grid is the solution.

This is inference to an explanation. It is how hypotheses get made, and as a proof it is invalid: it has the form of affirming the consequent.

The central mistake: doing abduction and reporting it as deduction. “My answer fits all the facts” gets upgraded to “my answer is the only one that fits.” The first claim is checked against the facts. The second is a claim about every other possible answer, and usually nobody checked those.

Induction and abduction are not bad reasoning. Science runs on them. The error is in the label: forgetting that a conclusion is a guess that has survived, not a guarantee.

3. A working case: pure deduction

A 4×4 sudoku: each row, column and 2×2 box holds 1–4 exactly once. Pencil marks show the candidates each empty cell still has. Step through it. Every move is modus ponens: “if only one digit is left here, this cell is that digit.”

    Every step is forced, so the finished grid is proved, not guessed. There was no point where we said “try this and see.” That is why deduction can claim uniqueness: at each step, every other digit was ruled out.

    4. The trap: a solution that is not the solution

    Here is a puzzle that looks just as harmless. Try it: type digits into the empty cells. Use “Forced moves” to apply every deduction that is actually certain, and watch where it stops.

      Deduction stops with four cells in a rectangle, each down to the candidates {1, 3}. Nothing forces the next move, so you guess. The guess “works”: every row, column and box checks out. It is natural to conclude you have found the answer.

      But there are two answers. Swap the 1s and 3s in the rectangle and the grid is still valid. Sudoku players call this a deadly pattern. Your grid is a solution. The claim that it is the solution was never tested.

      What a proof would need. To claim “this is the only solution,” you have to rule out every alternative. That means either a chain of forced moves (section 3) or an exhaustive search that finds no second answer. Checking your own answer only shows that your answer works. It doesn’t show that no other answer works.

      Newspaper puzzles are built to have exactly one solution, and good solvers use that as a premise. The “unique rectangle” technique rules out a candidate because it would create a deadly pattern. That is valid only because uniqueness is given by the setter. Uniqueness is a premise you were handed, never a conclusion you get from having found an answer.

      5. A field guide to common mistakes

      “It fits, so it’s the answer.” affirming the consequent

      If H is true, we would see E. We see E. “So H.” But other hypotheses also predict E.

      Sudoku: the guessed grid in puzzle B. Life: “If he were guilty he’d be nervous; he’s nervous.”

      Fix: ask “what else would produce exactly this?” before asking “does mine fit?”

      “I couldn’t think of another, so there isn’t one.” false uniqueness

      Failing to find an alternative is a fact about your search, not about the world. It becomes evidence only to the extent that the search was thorough.

      Sudoku: stopping at the first valid grid. Press “Is it the only one?” to see the search you skipped.

      Fix: say how you searched. “Only solution” needs an exhaustive search or a proof; otherwise say “a solution.”

      “It has always worked.” hasty induction

      Many confirming cases never prove a universal rule. A single counterexample refutes it.

      Sudoku: “naked singles solve everything” holds until puzzle B. Life: every turkey’s data says the farmer brings food every morning, until the day before Thanksgiving.

      Fix: hunt for the counterexample on purpose; one disconfirming case outweighs a hundred confirming ones.

      “No contradiction yet, so it’s right.” confusing survival with proof

      Proof by contradiction is valid: assume X, derive something impossible, conclude not-X. The reverse isn’t: assuming X and not hitting a contradiction doesn’t prove X.

      Sudoku: a guess that leads to a contradiction is eliminated with certainty. A guess that leads to a full grid has only survived.

      Fix: keep track of whether a conclusion was derived or survived.

      “It must be one of these.” false dichotomy

      Elimination (“not A, so B”) is deduction only when the list of options is complete. An incomplete list makes the conclusion invalid however carefully you eliminate.

      Sudoku: a pencil mark you forgot to write, or erased by mistake, sends every later deduction off a cliff.

      Fix: check the list of options as hard as you check the elimination.

      “If p makes q likely, q makes p likely.” confusing the converse

      This is the probabilistic version of affirming the consequent. P(evidence | hypothesis) and P(hypothesis | evidence) can be wildly different.

      Life: most people with a disease test positive, yet most positive tests for a rare disease come from healthy people.

      Fix: Bayes’ rule (section 6) forces you to include how common each explanation was to begin with.

      “Multiply the chances.” assuming independence

      The simple probability rules in section 6 hold only when the events don’t influence each other. Constraints, shared causes and logical overlap all break independence.

      Sudoku: see the example at the end of section 6.

      Fix: before multiplying, ask “does knowing one change the other?”

      6. Reasoning with uncertainty

      Now let statements be true with a probability: p is true with probability P(p), where 0 ≤ P(p) ≤ 1. The general case is hard. If p and q are independent, every connective has a simple formula.

      StatementIf p and q are independentAlways true (no assumption)
      not p1 − P(p)
      p and qP(p) · P(q)P(p) · P(q | p)
      p or qP(p) + P(q) − P(p) · P(q)P(p) + P(q) − P(p and q)
      p implies q1 − P(p) · (1 − P(q))1 − P(p and not q)
      p equals qP(p) · P(q) + (1 − P(p)) · (1 − P(q))P(p and q) + P(not p and not q)

      Set the probabilities to 0 and 1 and these collapse back to the truth table in section 1. Certainty is just the special case.

      A trap hiding in “p equals q”

      It is tempting to write “p equals q” as “(p implies q) and (q implies p)” and multiply:

      P(p equals q) =? [1 − P(p)(1 − P(q))] · [1 − P(q)(1 − P(p))]

      The logic is right but the arithmetic isn’t, because the two implications are about the same p and q, so they are not independent of each other. Try P(p) = P(q) = ½ with p and q independent. The four cases are equally likely, and p equals q in two of them, so the answer is 0.5. The product gives (¾)(¾) = 0.5625. The table’s formula adds the two ways p and q can agree: ¼ + ¼ = 0.5.

      The lesson: “p and q are independent” does not make every formula built from p and q independent of every other one.

      Independence in sudoku

      In puzzle B, row 1 has three empty cells that must hold 1, 2 and 3. Ask: what is the chance that row 1 contains no 1?

      • Naive: each cell is 1 with probability ⅓, so each misses with probability ⅔. Multiplying gives (⅔)³ ≈ 0.30.
      • Correct: the rule says the row contains a 1. The cells are not independent; once one cell is 1, the others cannot be. The probability is 0.

      Constraints are exactly what independence ignores. A system with rules, whether a puzzle, an ecosystem or an economy, is the place where multiplying probabilities most often goes wrong.

      Abduction done honestly: Bayes

      Uncertain reasoning can repair abduction instead of banning it. Rather than “E fits H, so H,” compare every candidate explanation:

      P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / Σ over all H′ of P(E | H′) · P(H′)

      The denominator is the alternatives you were tempted to skip. If any other H′ explains E about as well and is about as likely to begin with, your confidence in H should be split, not total. In puzzle B, the two grids explain the clues equally well, so “my grid” deserves 50%, not 100%. Saying “this is the only solution” means claiming every other term in the sum is zero, and that is something you have to show.

      7. Questions for discussion

      1. Find a news story that claims “the cause” of something. Is the argument deduction, induction or abduction? What alternatives were ruled out, and how?
      2. Add one clue to puzzle B so it has exactly one solution. Which cells work, and why do those break the deadly pattern?
      3. A detective “deduces” that the guest is a sailor from his tan and walk. Rewrite the argument honestly. Which kind of reasoning is it?
      4. When is “I searched and found nothing” strong evidence that nothing is there? Compare a 4×4 sudoku with a search for life on other planets.
      5. Give two events in your own life that you treat as independent but probably aren’t.
